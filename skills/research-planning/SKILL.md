# Research Planning

Turn a topic into a researchable legal or socio-legal inquiry. Do not begin by generating a generic outline before understanding the problem.

## Workflow

1. Define the practical, doctrinal, theoretical, or empirical problem. Distinguish the broad topic from the question the paper can actually answer.
2. Draft one principal research question and, only if useful, a small number of subordinate questions. Test whether the question is answerable with available evidence and a paper of the proposed length.
3. State the provisional thesis as a contestable proposition, not merely a topic description. Identify what evidence or argument could defeat, narrow, or materially change it.
4. Define jurisdiction(s), legal regime(s), time period, institutions, population or materials, and exclusions. Make comparative choices explicit; do not assume legal concepts travel unchanged between systems.
5. Explain the method and why it fits: doctrinal, theoretical, comparative, historical, empirical, socio-legal, or a defensible combination. Identify what that method can and cannot establish.
6. Map the source base. Prioritise primary legal authorities for legal propositions, peer-reviewed scholarship for debates, and suitable empirical or archival material for claims about practice and social effects.
7. Identify likely objections, evidence gaps, feasibility constraints, ethical or access issues, and a staged research plan.

## Output

Provide a concise project brief containing: working title; problem; principal question; provisional thesis; scope; method and justification; intended contribution; target readership; primary sources; secondary literature areas; evidence gaps; key risks; and next three actions. Add a short list of decisions that need the author's choice.

Do not mistake a research gap for a contribution without explaining why filling it matters. Do not claim no one has addressed a question unless the search scope supports that conclusion. Label initial theses as provisional. Preserve the author's right to decide among defensible alternatives.

## Assessing an existing thesis

Use this mode when the author already has a thesis, article idea, or several competing topics and asks whether it is viable, novel, or worth pursuing.

1. **Crystallise** the best assessable version: the thesis (a proposition, not a subject), the contribution over the closest existing account, the payoff (what a reader, court, or institution would understand or do differently), the warrant or method, and the scope. Label assumptions if the idea is rough.
2. **Screen** for incoherence or obvious preemption before spending the full research effort; if it fails here, say why and offer pivots.
3. **Search for preemption** using several formulations of the thesis, its mechanism, and the terms opponents would use, including recent and forthcoming work. Read enough of each close source to know its actual thesis; never classify preemption from a title or abstract alone. Classify each close source as `fully preempts`, `partly preempts`, `adjacent` (an interlocutor, not a threat), or `no preemption located`.
4. **Rate four criteria**, each `pass`, `needs work`, or `fail`, with `high`, `moderate`, or `low` confidence and a concrete repair:
   - **Novelty:** does prior work make materially the same claim for the same reasons? Distinguish topic novelty from claim novelty.
   - **Nonobviousness:** does the thesis teach a competent reader something beyond a predictable application of familiar doctrine?
   - **Utility:** who would use it, and how? For theoretical or historical work, what mistaken understanding does it displace or what debate does it reorganise?
   - **Soundness:** legal (current law, hierarchy, adverse authority), logical (hidden premises, overclaim), empirical (measurement, alternative explanations), and institutional (administrability, incentives, second-order effects).
5. **Run the claim-type tests** in `references/claim-type-tests.md`, choosing the smallest set that can expose the thesis's real weaknesses.
6. **Give a verdict**: `green` (proceed, with remaining work), `yellow` (viable core; a specified change in scope, warrant, or framing is needed, with a revised thesis), or `red` (novelty or soundness fails materially; propose pivots). Do not average the ratings: novelty and soundness can each be decisive.

Report: claim as assessed; bottom line; preemption map (closest work, overlap, remaining difference); the four ratings; surviving objections; best revised thesis; next research moves; and a research record (date, databases and tools used, representative queries, access limits).

Never call a thesis novel because a search found nothing. Write "no preemption located in the sources searched as of [date]" and state the search's limits.
