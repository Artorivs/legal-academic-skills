# Claim-type tests

Choose the smallest set that can expose a thesis's material weaknesses. Combine sets for mixed claims. For each test, state the scenario or challenge, apply the thesis, and say whether the result is acceptable. Used by `research-planning` when assessing a thesis and by `manuscript-review` when testing a manuscript's contribution.

## Doctrinal or normative

Use five to seven for a mature thesis:

1. **Core case:** the strongest intended application.
2. **Easy extension:** a nearby case testing useful reach.
3. **Hard case:** facts that expose ambiguity in the proposed rule or standard.
4. **Role reversal:** the same principle with the parties, states, or political valence reversed.
5. **Best counterexample:** the strongest fair case against the thesis.
6. **Institutional stress:** application with weak information, incentives, or capacity.
7. **Boundary case:** a case just outside the proposed rule.

Core-case failure may be fatal; role-reversal failure is a consistency problem; hard-case uncertainty is a drafting problem; an arbitrary boundary is a scope problem.

## Empirical or causal

Construct validity (does the measure capture the concept?); identification (causation or correlation?); alternative explanation; selection and missingness (who or what is absent from the data?); external validity (where should the result stop generalising?); triangulation (do other data or methods agree?); legal translation (even if true, does the finding support the legal conclusion?).

## Historical

Source survival (would lost sources change the account?); selection (were sources chosen because they fit?); anachronism (are modern categories imported?); representativeness (do elite texts or unusual disputes stand for ordinary practice?); rival account (can the same record support a different story?); change over time (are distinct periods flattened?); doctrinal payoff (does the history bear the interpretive weight placed on it?).

## Theoretical, interpretive, or taxonomic

Counterexample (what accepted case resists the account?); boundary (does the category include too much or too little?); explanatory gain (what does it explain better than rivals?); compression versus distortion (is simplicity bought by erasing relevant difference?); prediction or retrodiction (what should follow that rivals would not expect?); falsifier (what would make the author narrow or abandon it?); use (what can a later scholar do with it?).

## Comparative

Functional equivalence (do the compared institutions solve the same problem?); context (do constitutional structure, procedure, culture, or political economy explain the difference?); selection (why these jurisdictions or periods?); translation (do similar terms mask different legal functions?); transplantability (would the borrowing work under the receiving system's institutions?); negative case (does an omitted jurisdiction undermine the pattern?).

## Early-stage screen

For a hunch or seminar-paper idea, use three tests only: the strongest intended example; the strongest fair counterexample or rival account; and the boundary, measurement, or source problem most likely to defeat the project.
