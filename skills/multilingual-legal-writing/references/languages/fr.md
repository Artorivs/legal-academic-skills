# French

French is a language of several legal systems and of international courts. Name the system before choosing terms.

## Legal systems that use this language

France; Belgium; Luxembourg; Switzerland (with German and Italian); Quebec (civil law) and Canada (bijural federal law, English and French equally authentic); Monaco; OHADA member states; many other francophone African states. International: English and French are official languages of the ICJ and the European Court of Human Rights; French is one of the six UN official languages and a working language of the CJEU. UNVERIFIED in detail.

## Source hierarchy (France)

Constitution and *bloc de constitutionnalité*; treaties and EU law; *lois* and *ordonnances*; *règlements* (décrets, arrêtés); *circulaires* and guidance. Case law is formally not a source of law but is authoritative in practice. Separate administrative courts (Conseil d'État at the apex) from ordinary courts (Cour de cassation at the apex), and both from the Conseil constitutionnel. UNVERIFIED in detail.

## Primary-source databases

Légifrance (legislation and much case law); Judilibre (Cour de cassation open data); the Conseil d'État's own decision database; the Conseil constitutionnel's site; EUR-Lex for EU law. For other francophone systems, start from the national official gazette and the apex court's site.

## Scholarship databases

HAL and ISIDORE (open repositories); Cairn and OpenEdition (journals); Dalloz and LexisNexis (subscription). Resolve DOIs through Crossref for volume, issue, and pages; repositories often omit pagination.

## Citation conventions

No single national standard. Follow the target journal. Decisions are commonly identified by court, chamber, date, and number, with ECLI where available.

## Register

French legal scholarship (*doctrine*) is more willing to systematise and classify than common-law writing; keep that structure when translating into English rather than flattening it, and explain classifications English readers will not know.

## False friends and drift-prone terms

| Term | Apparent equivalent | Why it misleads | Better rendering |
|---|---|---|---|
| *jurisprudence* | jurisprudence | Means case law, not legal theory | case law |
| *doctrine* | doctrine | Means legal scholarship | scholarly commentary, legal scholarship |
| *arrêt* / *jugement* / *décision* | judgment | Different courts and stages | keep the distinction, e.g. *arrêt* (appellate or apex judgment) |
| *recours pour excès de pouvoir* | judicial review | Different grounds, standing, and remedies | keep the term with an explanatory gloss |
| *droit* | right / law | Both senses | choose by context |
| *Conseil d'État* | Council of State | The English calque obscures its dual advisory and judicial role | keep the French name |

## Formulaic prose to avoid

*Il convient de noter que*, *force est de constater que*, and *dans un premier temps… dans un second temps* used mechanically; empty *en définitive* conclusions. UNVERIFIED list; extend from a native reader's review.
